## COLD OPEN — 0:00–0:35

**[VISUAL: Globe spin → zoom into the North Atlantic → the coast of Newfoundland, Canada. No place name on screen yet.]**

Found just 25 kilometres off the coast of Canada, in the cold, foggy waters of the North Atlantic, you will find these two very interesting islands.

The islands in question are Saint-Pierre and Miquelon. And despite sitting closer to Canada than most Canadians live to a Tim Hortons, they are not Canadian. They're not a country either, and they're not really a colony.

They are, quite literally, France. People here drive Renaults, pay in euros, vote for the French president, and bake fresh baguettes every morning — 4,000 kilometres away from Paris.

So what on Earth is this place? Why does France still own it? And why is there a 200-mile-long shape on the map here that geographers call "the baguette"?

Let's swim across and investigate.

## SEGMENT 1 — WHERE ON EARTH IS IT? — 0:35–1:40

**[VISUAL: Map with distance lines to Newfoundland, Paris, and Miami.]**

So, as just mentioned, the islands sit in the North Atlantic, right below Newfoundland. Their closest point to Canada is about 25 kilometres — on a clear day, which to be fair isn't very often around here, you can literally see Canada from the shore. Mainland France, meanwhile, is some 4,300 kilometres away across the Atlantic.

The territory is made up of two main islands. Miquelon-Langlade is by far the bigger one, making up about 80% of the land. But here's the strange part — almost nobody lives there. Nearly everyone lives on Saint-Pierre, the tiny island in the south, which is roughly 25 kilometres squared. That's about the size of a single London borough.

Altogether, the territory covers around 242 kilometres squared. If it were a country, it would rank as one of the smallest on Earth — only microstates like Malta, the Maldives and Liechtenstein territory would keep it company at the bottom of the table.

## SEGMENT 2 — SO WHAT ON EARTH IS IT? — 1:40–3:00

**[VISUAL: French government org-chart graphic; tricolour overlays.]**

So if it's not part of Canada, not a country, and not a colony — what on Earth is it?

Well, officially, Saint-Pierre and Miquelon is classed as a "self-governing territorial overseas collectivity of the French Republic."

But what on Earth does that gibberish actually mean?

It means the islands are a full part of France — just like Normandy or Brittany — but with a local council that handles things like taxes, customs and urban planning. The people here are French citizens with French passports. They vote in French presidential elections, and they even send their own senator and deputy to the parliament in Paris.

In other words, while Canada is visible on the horizon, the law of the land here is French law. The police are French. The schools follow the French curriculum. And yes, the euro is the official currency — making this one of the very few places in North America where you can pay for your groceries with EU coins.

Here's where it gets even stranger, though. Despite being part of France, the islands are *not* part of the European Union proper — they're what Brussels calls an "overseas country and territory." So a piece of France, using the euro, sitting next to Canada, outside the EU. Simple, right?

And it's fair to say the islanders have a pretty sweet deal. Paris covers defence, healthcare and hefty subsidies, while the locals run their day-to-day affairs on their own schedule — quite literally, actually, because the islands even have their own time zone, 30 minutes ahead of Newfoundland and unique in all of North America.

## SEGMENT 3 — WHO LIVES THERE? — 3:00–4:00

**[VISUAL: Drone shots of Saint-Pierre town; colourful wooden houses.]**

The islands have a tiny population — only around 6,000 people call them home. That means the entire territory could fit inside two large cruise ships, with room to spare.

Around 90% of those people live in the town of Saint-Pierre, the capital, which honestly looks like someone picked up a Breton fishing village, painted it in bright Nordic colours, and dropped it into Canada.

The language here is, of course, French — but not Canadian French. Locals speak with an accent closer to metropolitan France, with hints of Norman and Basque, because that's exactly who settled here: fishermen from Normandy, Brittany and the Basque Country. To this day the town has a fronton — a traditional Basque pelota court — which is not something you expect to find 25 kilometres from Newfoundland.

## SEGMENT 4 — THE HISTORY BIT (AL CAPONE'S FAVOURITE ISLAND) — 4:00–5:40

**[VISUAL: Archive photos; Prohibition-era footage; whisky crates.]**

But why does France still own this place at all? Well, France once controlled a colossal chunk of North America — from Quebec all the way down to Louisiana. After losing wars with Britain in the 1700s, that empire was carved away piece by piece, until only one fragment remained: these two little islands, kept so French fishermen could keep working the cod-rich waters nearby. Saint-Pierre and Miquelon is, quite literally, the last surviving piece of New France.

And it's impossible to make a video about these islands without mentioning their golden age: Prohibition.

When the United States banned alcohol in 1920, this sleepy fishing outpost suddenly became the most important warehouse in the Atlantic. It was foreign soil, right next to North America, where alcohol was perfectly legal. Millions of bottles of whisky, champagne and rum flowed through the harbour, and smugglers — allegedly including Al Capone himself, who is said to have visited — ran them down the coast to thirsty Americans.

The islanders did rather well out of it. So much liquor passed through that locals built sheds — and in one famous case an entire villa — out of leftover wooden whisky crates.

Oh, and one more piece of history you genuinely won't believe: Saint-Pierre is home to the only guillotine ever used in North America. In 1889, a convicted murderer was executed here with a guillotine shipped over from Martinique. It's now in the local museum. Lovely.

---

## SEGMENT 5 — THE ECONOMY AND "THE BAGUETTE" — 5:40–7:00

**[VISUAL: Fishing boats; then the 1992 maritime boundary map.]**

So what do people actually do here today? Well, for centuries the answer was one word: cod. But when the cod stocks collapsed in the early 1990s and Canada shut down the fishery, the islands' entire economy went with it. These days the economy runs on public jobs, a growing trickle of tourism, and generous support from Paris. Take any exact GDP figure you see online with a pinch of salt — but per person, the French state spends more here than almost anywhere else in the Republic.

The fishing collapse also produced my favourite shape on any map, anywhere on Earth.

France and Canada spent years arguing over who owned the waters around the islands. In 1992, an international tribunal delivered its verdict — and it has to be seen to be believed. France got a small circle of sea around the islands… plus a corridor just 10.5 nautical miles wide but 200 nautical miles long, shooting straight south into the Atlantic.

Geographers immediately nicknamed it "the baguette." A long, thin strip of French waters, baked directly into the middle of Canada's exclusive economic zone. I mean, if you're going to lose a maritime dispute, at least lose it in the most French way possible.

---

## SEGMENT 6 — CLIMATE, VIBE AND THE FLAG — 7:00–8:10

**[VISUAL: Foggy coastline; seabirds; then the flag reveal.]**

Now, what's it actually like to be there? In one word: foggy. The cold Labrador Current meets the warm Gulf Stream right here, which blankets the islands in fog for a large part of the year. Summers are cool, winters are snowy, and the general vibe is rugged, windswept and raw — a French village with Canadian weather.

And of course, we have to look at the flag. Officially, the islands just fly the French tricolour. But the local flag is something else entirely: a golden ship — the Grande Hermine, which carried the explorer Jacques Cartier past these islands in 1536 — sailing on a blue sea, with three squares along the side representing the three peoples who settled here: the Basques, the Bretons and the Normans. Three flags within a flag. It's basically a history lesson you can hoist up a pole.
